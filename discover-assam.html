DISCOVER ASSAM

People. Culture.

An introduction to Assam — its administrative structure, geography, history, society, languages, cultural traditions and economic framework.

AT A GLANCE

Assam in brief.

Assam is a constituent state of India governed under a parliamentary system with a unicameral Legislative Assembly consisting of 126 seats.

Legislative Assembly126 seats

GOVERNANCE

01

Administrative structure.

State administration

  • Capital: Dispur
  • 35 administrative districts
  • Unicameral Legislative Assembly with 126 seats
  • Judiciary: Gauhati High Court, located in Guwahati

Autonomous councils

Bodoland Territorial Council (BTC), Karbi Anglong Autonomous Council (KAAC), and Dima Hasao Autonomous District Council (DHADC) operate under the Sixth Schedule of the Indian Constitution to grant administrative autonomy to indigenous tribal populations.

PHYSICAL GEOGRAPHY

River valleys, hills and monsoon landscapes.

Topography

Assam is divided into three primary zones: the Brahmaputra Valley in the north, the Barak Valley in the south, and the south-central hilly regions comprising the Karbi Anglong and Dima Hasao districts.

Hydrology

The Brahmaputra River runs east to west through the state for approximately 640 kilometres. It has an extreme braided channel morphology, carries a high sediment load, forms numerous river islands including Majuli, and causes severe seasonal flooding between June and September.

Climate

Assam has a humid subtropical climate with heavy monsoon rainfall averaging between 1,500 mm and more than 3,000 mm annually. Temperatures range from 6°C in winter to 38°C in summer.

Ecology

More than 30% of the state is under forest cover, ranging from tropical wet evergreen forests to riverine grasslands. Kaziranga National Park and Manas National Park are UNESCO World Heritage Sites and protect the world’s largest population of the great Indian one-horned rhinoceros.

HISTORICAL TIMELINE

Centuries of kingdoms, change and continuity.

4th–12th Century
Ancient Period

Governed as the Kamarupa Kingdom successively by the Varman, Mlechchha, and Pala dynasties from the capital of Pragjyotishpura, modern Guwahati.

1228–1826
Medieval Period

Dominated by the Ahom Dynasty, founded by King Sukaphaa. The Ahoms ruled for nearly 600 years, developed the Paik system, and repelled 17 military campaigns by the Mughal Empire, including the Battle of Saraighat in 1671. The Koch, Kachari and Chutia kingdoms also controlled parts of the region.

1826–1947
Colonial Period

Following the First Anglo-Burmese War, the British Empire annexed the territory under the Treaty of Yandaboo in 1826. Commercial tea plantations, coal mining, and Asia’s first commercial oil well at Digboi in 1889 developed during this period.

Post-1947
Post-Independence

Assam became an Indian state in 1950. Nagaland, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh were subsequently carved out of its original territory to provide political autonomy to distinct ethnic tribal populations.

DEMOGRAPHICS & SOCIETY

A pluralistic mosaic.

Assam’s demographic landscape is fundamentally rural, with approximately 85% of its 35 million residents living in villages, hamlets and agricultural settlements. Urbanization is concentrated primarily in Guwahati, Dibrugarh, Silchar, Jorhat and Nagaon.

Its population reflects centuries of migration and assimilation across Indo-Aryan, Tibeto-Burman and Austroasiatic linguistic and ancestral lineages.

PEOPLE & COMMUNITIES

Many communities, distinct traditions.

Non-Tribal Plains Population

Includes various caste Hindu communities such as Kalitas and Brahmins and Assamese Muslims, primarily inhabiting the fertile Brahmaputra Valley.

Plains Tribes

Includes Bodos, Misings, Rabhas, Sonowal Kacharis and Tiwas, each maintaining distinct social institutions and traditional village councils.

Hill Tribes

Includes Karbis, Dimasas and sections of Kuki and Zeme Naga communities, with customary laws protected under the Sixth Schedule of the Indian Constitution.

Tea Tribes

Descendants of indentured labourers brought by British planters from central and eastern India, including communities such as Santhals, Mundas, Oraons and Gonds.

Barak Valley Populations

The southern plains around the Barak River are predominantly inhabited by Sylheti-speaking Bengalis, alongside indigenous tribal pockets including Bishnupriya Manipuri and Khasi communities.

LANGUAGES & DIALECTS

A multilingual state.

Assamese (Asamiya)

The primary official language and lingua franca across the diverse communities of the Brahmaputra Valley.

Bodo

A Tibeto-Burman language with co-official status and the primary administrative language within the Bodoland Territorial Council area.

Bengali

The official language in the southern Barak Valley districts, where the local population predominantly speaks the Sylheti dialect.

Tribal Languages

Karbi, Dimasa and Mising remain widely spoken across hill districts and the upper valley.

Sadri

A creole language blending elements of Hindi, Odia, Bengali and tribal languages, widely used among Tea Tribe communities.

RELIGIOUS COMPOSITION

A diverse spiritual landscape.

Hinduism — 61.5%

Widely practiced by plains and tribal communities, with strong traditions of Neo-Vaishnavism, Shaktism centred around Kamakhya Temple, and Shaivism.

Islam — 34.2%

Includes indigenous Assamese Muslim communities and a large Bengali-speaking Muslim population shaped by colonial and post-colonial migration.

Christianity — 3.7%

Practiced predominantly among hill tribes and sections of the Tea Tribes.

Minorities

Historically significant Buddhist, Sikh and Jain communities also reside in Assam, especially in urban and Upper Assam areas.

CULTURAL TRADITIONS

Bihu, Sattriya, weaving and shared identity.

RONGALI / BOHAG BIHU

Spring & New Year

Celebrated in mid-April to mark spring, the Assamese New Year and seeding time, with energetic folk dances, Dhol and Pepa.

KONGALI / KATI BIHU

Protection of crops

A solemn mid-October observance when earthen lamps are lit near Tulsi plants and in paddy fields.

BHOGALI / MAGH BIHU

Harvest & feasting

Celebrated in mid-January with community feasting, Mejis and traditional sports.

The Sattriya Institution

Founded by Srimanta Sankardev, the Sattra system preserved Sattriya Nritya, Ankia Naat and Borgeet while promoting an egalitarian form of Vaishnavism.

Handloom & Textiles

Weaving is a major cultural marker and rural household industry, especially known for Muga, Eri and Pat silks and traditional attire such as Mekhela Chador.

ECONOMIC FRAMEWORK

Agriculture, tea and energy.

Agriculture

Employs approximately 70% of the workforce. Rice is the principal food crop, with jute, mustard, sugarcane and pulses also important.

Tea Sector

Assam is the world’s largest contiguous tea-growing region and produces more than 50% of India’s total tea output across estates and smallholdings.

Hydrocarbons

Assam has substantial crude oil and natural gas fields, with refineries at Digboi, Guwahati, Bongaigaon and Numaligarh.
